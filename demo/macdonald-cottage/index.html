How it works

Charlie built the cottage for himself and Mabel. It's small, unconventional, quirky and fun.

One guest called it 4+ star camping.

What you get

  • Hot and cold running water, gravity fed from a spring in the forest
  • An indoor flush toilet
  • An undercover, on-demand hot water shower in the car port
  • An electric stove and refrigerator, plus a wood stove to take off a chill
  • Pots and pans, and dishes, and cutlery and charm
  • Two beds upstairs, one full size and one three-quarter size, with bedding for two

What to know before you come

  • The beds are reached by a tight spiral staircase, a particular challenge for anyone with mobility issues.
  • There is no wi-fi. We have installed a cellular booster in the cottage, and there is service at the beach fifty metres away.
  • The shower is outdoors, under cover.
  • Stays are two nights or more.

Charlie and Mabel

Charles William Macdonald (1874 to 1967) was a seaman, labourer, artist and manufacturer. He and Mabel had camped at Huntington Point since 1919.

In the 1930s he purchased land, and when business slowed at his Kentville Concrete, he and his worker partners went to the shore to see what they could build. To prove its durability as a building medium, they fashioned five cottages using concrete with stone and wood.

Of the five cottages, four remain. The Teapot was unceremoniously bulldozed into the Bay in the 1980s.

Charlie's favourite has remained in the family. To help with its upkeep, the Macdonalds are offering an uncommon engagement with Nova Scotia heritage.

To learn more about Charlie's concrete sculptures and story, be sure to visit the Charles Macdonald Concrete Museum in nearby Centreville.

Huntington Point

The beach is fifty metres away. It is popular for finding unique rocks, beach bonfires, summertime picnics, and watching the highest tides on Earth.
